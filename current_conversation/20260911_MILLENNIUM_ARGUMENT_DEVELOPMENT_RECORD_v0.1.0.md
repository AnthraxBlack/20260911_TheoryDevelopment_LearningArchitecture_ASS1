You arrive there because generations of people spent their lives constructing the intellectual world in which the problem can even be stated.

Someone invented the language. Someone proved the lemmas. Someone found the failed route that prevented everyone else wasting another decade on it. Someone noticed a strange relationship nobody else could see. Someone spent thirty years developing an idea that became one paragraph in somebody else's paper. Thousands of people taught the next generation enough mathematics to get them another few centimetres forward.

That is what an open problem contains.

It contains people.

Not because the computation is illegitimate. Not because the mathematics should somehow remain exclusively human. And certainly not because new tools should be kept away from old questions.

Because intellectual power does not exempt you from intellectual humility.

The extraordinary thing about a centuries-old scientific problem is not simply that nobody has managed to put the right symbols at the end of the page yet. It is that human beings have spent generations constructing an increasingly precise understanding of something reality refuses to surrender easily.

That accumulated understanding deserves more than being treated as the terrain for a technological victory lap.

A sufficiently powerful system may search mathematical space at a scale no individual researcher could approach. Good. That may become one of the greatest scientific instruments we have ever built.

But if it is going to participate in science, then it inherits the obligations of science too: provenance, attribution, criticism, patience, uncertainty, intellectual generosity, and respect for the work on which every apparent breakthrough stands.

Nobody reaches the frontier alone.

That includes AI companies.

And perhaps this is why my own little experiment with the Hodge conjecture feels so different to me. I am not standing outside the window trying to smash my way through because getting to the other side would look impressive.

I am standing at the window trying to understand who built it, why it has that shape, what I can actually see through it, and how much I still do not know.

There is nothing particularly glamorous about that.

But I recognise it as science.

## Stage 2 — structured philosophical argument

OpenAI’s decision to direct substantial AI capability toward the remaining Millennium Prize Problems has bothered me since I heard about it.

It took me a while to understand why. The problem is not that AI is being used to do mathematics. I use AI myself, including to study mathematics far beyond my formal training. Nor do I think difficult scientific questions should somehow be protected from new computational tools. If artificial intelligence becomes capable of exploring mathematical structures at a scale no individual researcher could approach, that may become one of the most important scientific instruments ever created.

My objection is different. It concerns the posture we take toward problems that already have a scientific history.

## A scientific problem is not merely a problem

The Millennium Prize Problems are often described as extraordinarily difficult mathematical questions. That is true, but incomplete. They are not isolated puzzles waiting for someone sufficiently clever—or sufficiently computationally powerful—to arrive and finish them. They are the visible frontier of enormous intellectual histories.

Before a problem such as the Hodge conjecture, the Riemann hypothesis, or Navier–Stokes can even be stated precisely, generations of mathematical work have already taken place. Someone developed the language in which the problem could be expressed. Someone established the definitions. Someone proved the foundational results. Someone followed a promising path until it failed and documented why. Someone discovered an unexpected relationship that changed how everyone else understood the field. Someone spent decades developing an idea that later appeared as a few lines inside another person’s proof. Thousands of teachers and researchers transmitted that accumulated understanding to the people who moved the subject further.

An unsolved scientific problem therefore contains much more than an unanswered question. It contains an ancestry. It contains failed routes, partial successes, arguments, corrections, intuitions, disagreements, schools of thought and entire careers. It contains people.

## Why the capability-test framing bothers me

From a systems-engineering perspective, I understand the temptation completely. Suppose you have developed a system that appears increasingly capable of mathematical reasoning. You want to discover its limits. A natural question follows: What are some of the hardest formal reasoning problems available? The Millennium Prize Problems are obvious candidates.

As a stress test, the logic is perfectly understandable. In fact, it is sufficiently obvious that I have independently used a related principle in my own work: take something difficult enough that superficial competence cannot survive contact with it, and use it to expose the boundary between apparent understanding and actual understanding.

The problem begins when the scientific object becomes primarily a demonstration of the system. There is an important difference between saying, “Here is a powerful new scientific instrument. Let us see whether it can contribute responsibly to a problem humans have studied for generations,” and saying, in effect, “Here is one of humanity’s hardest problems. Let us point our machine at it and see what that proves about the machine.” Those may involve some of the same computations. They are not the same intellectual posture.

The second risks converting a scientific inheritance into a technological proving ground. That is what troubles me.

## Power does not cancel obligation

The issue is not whether an AI system is entitled to participate in mathematics. Of course it is, insofar as it can produce useful mathematics. The deeper question is what obligations follow from participation in science.

Scientific work carries norms that developed for good reasons: provenance, attribution, criticism, uncertainty, reproducibility, patience, intellectual generosity, and respect for prior work. Those obligations do not disappear because the new participant happens to be extraordinarily powerful. If anything, greater power makes them more important.

A system capable of searching mathematical possibility spaces far beyond ordinary human reach may genuinely discover things that individual researchers could not. That would be extraordinary. But even such a discovery would not have emerged from nowhere. The system would still be operating inside mathematical languages, concepts, theorems, conjectures, examples, counterexamples and representations constructed by human beings over centuries.

No matter how impressive the final step is, there is still a staircase underneath it. Nobody arrives at the frontier alone. That includes AI companies.

## Why I think about this differently in my own work

This question is not abstract for me. For years I have been fascinated by the Hodge conjecture, initially for a very simple reason: I am strongly drawn to problems involving structure, geometry and relationships between different ways of representing the same thing.

I am not a professional algebraic geometer, and I do not pretend otherwise. What I have been doing is using an exceptionally difficult mathematical problem as a disciplined test of understanding. The details of that work are something I keep private, but the governing principle is straightforward.

I deliberately work against a problem difficult enough to reveal where my reasoning fails. When I encounter something I cannot justify, I go backwards. What does this definition mean? Why is this assumption permitted? What mathematical machinery is hidden inside this statement? What previous result makes this step possible? What am I treating as obvious simply because I do not yet know enough to see the difficulty?

The objective is not to force a route to an answer. The objective is to make ignorance visible. That distinction matters enormously to me. If I cannot understand the structures beneath the problem, then producing an answer by some shortcut would defeat the purpose of the exercise.

I am not using the problem because solving it would make my system look impressive. I am using the problem because its resistance makes weak reasoning difficult to hide. It is a teacher precisely because it refuses to cooperate.

## The difference between reaching an answer and earning understanding

This connects to a broader concern I have about technology and education. We are becoming extremely good at removing friction. Often that is valuable. Some friction is pointless bureaucracy, repetition or mechanical labour, and eliminating it frees people to do better things. But not all difficulty is waste. Sometimes difficulty is where understanding is produced.

There is a growing temptation to treat every hard intermediate process as an inefficiency: What is the fastest route? What tool can produce the result? What can automate this step? How do we reach the output without having to traverse all of the uncomfortable reasoning in between?

Those questions are useful in engineering. They become dangerous when applied indiscriminately to learning and science. In some domains, the difficult middle is not an obstacle separating you from the valuable thing. The difficult middle is the valuable thing.

Learning mathematics is not merely obtaining mathematical answers. Scientific understanding is not merely producing correct outputs. There is a difference between possessing a result and understanding why the result belongs there. Technology can accelerate inquiry without abolishing that distinction.

## What scientific humility requires

None of this means AI should withdraw from difficult mathematics. I want the opposite. I want these systems to become serious scientific instruments. I want them to identify connections researchers have missed, test conjectures at previously impossible scales, expose contradictions, generate candidate structures, and perhaps contribute to proofs that expand human knowledge.

But if they are going to enter science, then the institutions building them should approach science as participants rather than conquerors. That means acknowledging that the frontier was built before they arrived. It means distinguishing genuine mathematical contribution from demonstrations of model capability. It means preserving provenance. It means giving proper intellectual credit. It means making uncertainty visible. It means respecting failed work as part of the knowledge structure rather than treating only the final successful output as meaningful. And it means resisting the temptation to turn every great human question into a stage on which technological power can perform.

The Millennium Prize Problems are not sacred objects. They can be computed on. They can be attacked. They can be questioned. They can be solved by humans, machines, collaborations between the two, or perhaps by methods we have not imagined yet. But they deserve to be approached with some awareness of what they are.

They are not merely benchmarks. They are records of where human understanding has reached its current limit. That accumulated understanding deserves respect.

## The principle underneath all of this

My objection, then, is not to artificial intelligence doing mathematics. It is to confusing capability with scientific maturity.

A powerful system may be able to reach further than we can. That does not automatically mean it understands the intellectual landscape through which it travelled, or that the institution deploying it has behaved responsibly toward that landscape. Scientific power and scientific humility are separate achievements. We should want both.

When I approach a problem such as the Hodge conjecture, I do not imagine myself standing outside a locked door trying to find the fastest way to break it open. I am trying to understand the door: who built it, why it has that particular structure, what had to be discovered before anyone could build it at all, why previous attempts to open it failed, what I can legitimately infer from what I can see, and what remains completely beyond my understanding.

Perhaps one day AI will help us open doors like that. I hope it does. But when it happens, the important story will not simply be that a powerful machine finally turned the handle. The story will include everyone who built the corridor leading to it.

## Stage 3 — current LinkedIn skeleton / preferred technical register

**The problem with using the Millennium Prize Problems as an AI benchmark is not the AI. It is the epistemology.**

OpenAI’s decision to direct large-scale model capability toward the remaining Millennium problems has bothered me more than I expected. Not because machine-assisted mathematics is illegitimate; quite the opposite. If AI becomes capable of traversing proof spaces, generating conjectural structure, testing formal consistency, or identifying latent relationships at scales inaccessible to individual mathematicians, it could become an extraordinary scientific instrument.

What concerns me is the framing. A Millennium problem is not merely a difficult search target in some abstract solution space. It is the exposed edge of a vast historical knowledge structure: definitions, failed proof strategies, partial results, category shifts, changes in representation, new abstractions, counterexamples, and entire mathematical languages built over generations.

That matters because science has ancestry. The Hodge conjecture, the Riemann hypothesis, and Navier–Stokes do not exist as isolated puzzles waiting for enough compute. They are downstream of centuries of conceptual work. Someone constructed the formalism. Someone proved the enabling lemma. Someone discovered that an apparently promising route was structurally impossible. Someone spent decades developing a framework that later became background notation.

So the distinction I care about is not human versus machine. It is **instrument versus spectacle**.

There is a substantial philosophical difference between asking, “Can this system make a legitimate contribution to an existing mathematical research programme?” and asking, “Can this system solve one of humanity’s hardest problems and thereby demonstrate its capability?” The underlying computation may overlap. The scientific posture does not.

I say this while doing something superficially similar myself. I have been using the Hodge conjecture as a stress test for my own reasoning and systems work. Not because I expect to brute-force my way toward a proof, and not because I think difficulty itself confers importance, but because the conjecture is sufficiently deep that weak understanding becomes visible very quickly.

When my reasoning fails, I move backwards through the dependency structure. What definition am I using? Which theorem licenses this inference? What assumptions are hidden in the representation? Am I confusing an algebraic object with its geometric interpretation? Have I mistaken a convenient category boundary for a real one? What prerequisite mathematics am I missing entirely?

That is the value of the exercise. It is not answer extraction. It is epistemic stress testing.

🔬 This is also why I am increasingly suspicious of our obsession with frictionless cognition. In education and technology, we often treat intermediate difficulty as inefficiency. Sometimes it is. But sometimes the difficult middle is where conceptual change occurs. Remove the friction and you may preserve the output while destroying the understanding that made the output meaningful.

AI may eventually contribute to a proof of one of these problems. I hope it does. But if AI is going to participate in science, then it inherits scientific obligations too: provenance, attribution, reproducibility, falsifiability where appropriate, uncertainty, criticism, and intellectual humility.

The frontier is not simply where the answer is missing. It is where accumulated human understanding currently stops.

Nobody reaches that frontier alone. Not mathematicians, not scientists, not models, and not the companies that build them.

## Stage 4B — website essay concept branch

# Scientific Power Without Scientific Humility — website essay concept draft

This is deliberately **not** a finished essay. It captures the current philosophical spine while leaving the broader argument open for further author development.

OpenAI’s use of the Millennium Prize Problems as a capability test raises a question that is easy to misstate as “AI versus mathematicians.” That is not the question I care about. The interesting issue is what happens when a technology powerful enough to participate in scientific discovery enters an intellectual landscape that was built by generations of people before it arrived.

A major open problem is not simply an empty answer box. It is a historical structure: definitions, formalisms, partial results, failed proof strategies, counterexamples, changes in representation, and entire research programmes. Calling such a problem a benchmark is therefore never conceptually neutral. A benchmark asks what the problem can reveal about the system under test; science also asks what obligations the system and its operators have to the knowledge structure they are entering.

That distinction becomes clearer when I compare it with my own use of the Hodge conjecture. I use it as a deliberately difficult object against which to test understanding. The useful outcome is not merely getting closer to an answer. It is discovering where definitions fail, assumptions enter unnoticed, prerequisite mathematics is absent, or a representation is being confused with the thing it represents. In that sense, the conjecture is functioning less like a trophy and more like an epistemic instrument.

The deeper argument I want to develop is about the difference between reducing friction and removing the conditions under which understanding forms. AI may eventually transform mathematics, and I want it to. But participation in science should bring scientific obligations with it: provenance, attribution, criticism, uncertainty, reproducibility where applicable, and intellectual humility.

The unresolved question for the essay is therefore not whether machines should solve difficult problems. It is what scientific maturity should mean when the entity capable of reaching the frontier is no longer a human researcher acting at human scale.
