## Draft

### Why OpenAI's Millennium Prize Experiment Has Been Bothering Me

OpenAI’s decision to turn the Millennium Prize Problems into a large-scale capability test has been bothering me all day, and I think I finally worked out why.

It is not really about AI. AI is a tool. I use it myself. I use it to study mathematics that is miles beyond my formal training, and I have actually been using the Hodge conjecture as a kind of stress test. Not because I seriously think I am going to casually solve the Hodge conjecture, but because it is hard enough that I cannot bullshit my way through it.

Every time I think I understand something, the problem forces me backwards. What does that definition actually mean? Where did that assumption come from? What theorem is being used here? Why is this object defined that way? What mathematics do I need to learn before I am even entitled to have an opinion about this step? That is the point. I am using the problem to expose where my reasoning stops.

And maybe that is why OpenAI’s Millennium exercise got under my skin so badly. There is a very obvious systems-testing logic behind it. You build a system that appears to be getting very good at formal reasoning. You want to find its limits. So you ask: what are some of the hardest reasoning problems humans have? Well, there are the Millennium Prize Problems.

From a benchmarking perspective, I completely understand the move. It is probably exactly the sort of thing I would think of. But there is another part of this that matters to me much more, and it has almost nothing to do with AI. It is about respect for the people who came before you.

Science has an ancestry. You do not simply arrive at Hodge, Navier–Stokes, Riemann or any of the other great unresolved problems as though somebody left a particularly difficult puzzle sitting on a table and everyone has been too stupid to solve it. Those problems exist inside intellectual worlds that generations of human beings spent their lives constructing.

Someone invented the language. Someone proved the lemma that everybody now uses in three words. Someone followed a dead end far enough to prove that nobody else needed to waste another decade doing it. Someone noticed a relationship nobody had seen before. Someone spent thirty years developing an idea that eventually became a paragraph in somebody else’s paper. Thousands of people taught the next generation enough mathematics to move the frontier another few centimetres.

That is what an open problem contains. It contains people.

There is something about taking one of those enormous, historically accumulated scientific problems, throwing industrial-scale computation at it, and then presenting the result partly as, “Look what our technology can do,” that strikes me as intellectually backwards. Not because the computation is illegitimate. Not because mathematics should somehow remain exclusively human. And definitely not because we should keep powerful new tools away from old scientific questions.

Use them. Absolutely use them. If AI becomes capable of exploring mathematical spaces at scales no individual researcher could ever approach, that could become one of the greatest scientific instruments we have ever built. But intellectual power does not exempt you from intellectual humility.

If you are going to participate in science, you inherit the obligations of science as well: provenance, attribution, criticism, patience, uncertainty, intellectual generosity, and respect for the work underneath whatever result you eventually produce. Nobody arrives at the frontier alone. That includes AI companies.

There is another reason this hit me personally. I have had the Hodge conjecture floating around in my head for probably a decade, for an extremely sophisticated mathematical reason: I like shapes. That is basically how it started.

There was something about the problem that grabbed me years ago, and eventually I decided that if I was going to keep thinking about it, I should actually learn enough mathematics to understand what the hell it was saying. So my own little experiment with Hodge is also a systems test. I work with logic. I build systems. Of course I eventually thought: if I want to test whether my way of reasoning is any good, why not point it at something brutally difficult?

The basic instinct is not completely different. The difference is what I think the problem is for. I am not standing outside the window trying to smash my way through because getting to the other side would look impressive. I am standing at the window trying to understand who built it, why it has that shape, what mathematics makes the glass possible, what I can actually see through it, what I am merely imagining that I can see, and how much I still do not know.

If one day that process somehow contributed something useful to the actual mathematics, fantastic. But even if it never does, the problem has already done something valuable. It has taught me where I am ignorant. It has forced me to learn.

That matters because I think we are developing a much broader cultural problem with technology and education: increasingly, everything is treated as an obstacle to route around. What is the fastest path? What is the shortcut? What tool will produce the output? How do I get to the answer without having to do all of that annoying understanding in the middle?

Sometimes efficiency is exactly what we need. But sometimes the work in the middle is the entire point. There are things you should not want to bypass because the process of understanding them is what changes you. That is true in education. It is true in science. And I think it should remain true even when our tools become extraordinarily powerful. Maybe especially then.

The machine may be new. The frontier is not.

# Stage 3 - Integrated Philosophical Position

## Transformation
Stage 1 + Stage 2 amendments -> ordered argumentative structure.

## Structural order

1. AI is not the objection.
2. A scientific problem is not merely a problem.
3. Why capability-test framing is philosophically different.
4. Scientific power creates rather than erases obligations.
5. The author's own Hodge work as a contrasting epistemic practice.
6. The distinction between output and understanding.
7. Scientific humility as a requirement for AI participation.
8. Final principle: nobody reaches the frontier alone.

## Core integrated thesis

The strongest version of the argument is not "AI should not solve Millennium Prize Problems." It is that mathematical contribution, benchmark performance, and scientific maturity are distinct categories. A system may display enormous reasoning capability while the institution deploying it still adopts a poor scientific posture. The relevant standard is not whether a machine is allowed to contribute, but whether the surrounding practice respects provenance, attribution, accumulated intellectual labour, uncertainty, and the distinction between instrument and spectacle.

### Draft

**The problem with using the Millennium Prize Problems as an AI benchmark is not the AI. It is the epistemology.**

OpenAI’s decision to direct large-scale model capability toward the remaining Millennium problems has bothered me more than I expected. Not because machine-assisted mathematics is illegitimate; quite the opposite. If AI becomes capable of traversing proof spaces, generating conjectural structure, testing formal consistency, or identifying latent relationships at scales inaccessible to individual mathematicians, it could become an extraordinary scientific instrument.

What concerns me is the framing. A Millennium problem is not merely a difficult search target in some abstract solution space. It is the exposed edge of a vast historical knowledge structure: definitions, failed proof strategies, partial results, category shifts, changes in representation, new abstractions, counterexamples, and entire mathematical languages built over generations.

That matters because science has ancestry. The Hodge conjecture, the Riemann hypothesis, and Navier–Stokes do not exist as isolated puzzles waiting for enough compute. They are downstream of centuries of conceptual work. Someone constructed the formalism. Someone proved the enabling lemma. Someone discovered that an apparently promising route was structurally impossible. Someone spent decades developing a framework that later became background notation.

So the distinction I care about is not human versus machine. It is **instrument versus spectacle**. There is a substantial philosophical difference between asking, “Can this system make a legitimate contribution to an existing mathematical research programme?” and asking, “Can this system solve one of humanity’s hardest problems and thereby demonstrate its capability?” The underlying computation may overlap. The scientific posture does not.

I say this while doing something superficially similar myself. I have been using the Hodge conjecture as a stress test for my own reasoning and systems work. Not because I expect to brute-force my way toward a proof, and not because I think difficulty itself confers importance, but because the conjecture is sufficiently deep that weak understanding becomes visible very quickly.

When my reasoning fails, I move backwards through the dependency structure. What definition am I using? Which theorem licenses this inference? What assumptions are hidden in the representation? Am I confusing an algebraic object with its geometric interpretation? Have I mistaken a convenient category boundary for a real one? What prerequisite mathematics am I missing entirely?

That is the value of the exercise. It is not answer extraction. It is epistemic stress testing.

AI may eventually contribute to a proof of one of these problems. I hope it does. But if AI is going to participate in science, then it inherits scientific obligations too: provenance, attribution, reproducibility, falsifiability where appropriate, uncertainty, criticism, and intellectual humility.

The frontier is not simply where the answer is missing. It is where accumulated human understanding currently stops. Nobody reaches that frontier alone: not mathematicians, not scientists, not models, and not the companies that build them.

## 4B - Website Essay Seed

### Publication purpose
A restrained long-form seed, intentionally incomplete. This is not the final essay. It preserves enough structure to support later author-led expansion without prematurely fixing the argument.

### Working title
**Scientific Power Without Scientific Humility**

OpenAI’s use of the Millennium Prize Problems as a large-scale capability test raises a question that is easy to misstate. The issue is not whether AI should be allowed to do mathematics. That question is already obsolete. The more important question is what happens when a new form of intellectual power enters a scientific tradition whose objects, language, methods, failures, and standards were built long before that power existed.

A Millennium Prize Problem is not merely an unanswered formal proposition. It is the visible boundary of a historical knowledge system. Hodge, Riemann, and Navier–Stokes sit on top of accumulated definitions, proof techniques, abandoned approaches, counterexamples, abstractions, pedagogical lineages, and conceptual shifts. Their difficulty cannot be separated cleanly from that ancestry, because the very ability to state the problems depends on it.

This is why the framing of AI participation matters. A model may be used as an instrument inside mathematics, or a mathematical frontier may be used as an instrument for demonstrating the model. These are not equivalent acts, even when some of the computation overlaps. One treats the system as entering an existing research programme; the other risks treating the research programme as a stage for technological capability.

My own engagement with the Hodge conjecture makes that distinction personally uncomfortable rather than merely theoretical. I also use extreme difficulty as a stress test. But the purpose is not to bypass the intellectual structure surrounding the problem. It is to expose where my own representation fails, then move backward through definitions, assumptions, theorems, and missing prerequisites until I can see the failure more clearly. The private mechanics of that work are not relevant here; the epistemic orientation is.

The larger essay still needs to develop several unresolved questions: whether scientific norms can be meaningfully inherited by non-human systems or only by the institutions that deploy them; how provenance should work when mathematical outputs are generated from enormous training corpora; whether benchmark culture systematically mislocates scientific achievement; and how much of contemporary “friction removal” is actually the removal of the cognitive work through which understanding forms.

The central claim, however, is already stable: capability and scientific maturity are not the same achievement. If AI becomes a serious participant in mathematics, greater power should increase rather than diminish the demands for attribution, provenance, uncertainty, criticism, and humility.
